Programming, Gatekeeping and the Zero-Sum Mindset

The software world is often described as open, collaborative and meritocratic. From the outside, however, getting into it can feel strangely closed.

Programming has an unusual reputation. Almost everything appears to be available: source code, documentation, tutorials, forums, books and entire university courses can be found online. In theory, very little is hidden.

And yet access to information is not the same thing as access to understanding.

A beginner can open technical documentation and technically have the answer in front of them while still having no idea what it means. A method signature can assume familiarity with types, objects, inheritance, callbacks, asynchronous execution and conventions that nobody has explained yet. The information is public, but the background required to understand it is not automatically transmitted with it.

That distinction matters because software culture frequently treats the existence of information as proof that the knowledge itself is accessible.

The difference between difficult and unnecessarily cryptic

Some difficulty is unavoidable. Computers are complicated, abstractions build on other abstractions and there is no realistic way to reduce an entire technical field to a few simple explanations.

But difficulty and obscurity are not the same thing.

Technical communities can develop their own shorthand, assumptions and rituals. Once someone has spent years inside that environment, those conventions begin to feel obvious. They stop noticing how much unstated knowledge is required to follow a conversation.

This is where a strange response to beginners sometimes appears: if you do not understand the explanation, the conclusion is that you did not read enough, search enough or try hard enough.

When knowing something becomes part of someone's status, explaining it clearly can feel less valuable than displaying the fact that they know it.

That is where difficulty can turn into gatekeeping.

Knowledge as status

Technical ability naturally creates differences in experience. Someone who has programmed for fifteen years will know things that someone on their first month simply cannot know yet.

There is nothing wrong with that.

The problem begins when the difference in knowledge becomes a social hierarchy rather than a practical one.

You can see traces of this whenever asking a basic question is treated as embarrassing, when an explanation becomes an opportunity to demonstrate superiority, or when obscure terminology is used where ordinary language would have communicated the same idea more clearly.

At that point technical competence is no longer being used only to solve a problem. It is also being used to establish rank.

The zero-sum mindset

What interests me most is the competitive mentality behind some of this behavior.

It is a zero-sum way of looking at other people: if someone else gains an advantage, I have lost one. If somebody learns what I know, what I know becomes less valuable. If another person gets the position, recognition or opportunity, there is less available for me.

Under that logic, helping another person is not automatically a good thing. Sharing knowledge can feel like giving away an advantage.

Competition itself is not the problem. Competition can push people to improve. The problem is when success becomes tied to someone else's failure.

Instead of:

I want to become better at this.

the mentality becomes:

I need to know more than you do.

Those statements look similar, but they lead to very different cultures.

Is technology really a meritocracy?

The technology industry also likes the idea of meritocracy: learn the skill, demonstrate that you are capable and the opportunities will follow.

Reality is more complicated.

People enter the field with radically different starting points. One person may have grown up around computers, programmers and people who can answer technical questions. Another may encounter programming for the first time as an adult and have to discover even the basic vocabulary alone.

Connections matter too. Knowing somebody already inside an industry can provide context, recommendations, feedback and opportunities that are difficult to reproduce by simply reading documentation.

That does not mean everyone in software got there through nepotism. It also does not mean skill is irrelevant.

It means that calling a system purely meritocratic can hide the enormous difference between having access to information and having access to people who know how to interpret it.

Open source does not automatically mean open knowledge

Software has one of the strongest cultures of public collaboration of almost any technical field. Huge amounts of code are available for anyone to inspect.

But code is an artifact, not a teacher.

Looking at a mature codebase can show you how experienced developers built something. It does not necessarily explain why they built it that way, which alternatives they rejected, which concepts you are expected to know beforehand or how they learned those concepts in the first place.

For somebody who already understands the foundations, open source can be incredibly educational. For somebody who does not, it can look like thousands of lines written in a language they technically recognize but cannot actually follow.

Again, the material is open. The path through the material is not.

The part I find contradictory

This is what makes the culture around programming interesting to me.

It can be extraordinarily collaborative and strangely elitist at the same time.

People publish tools for free, answer questions from complete strangers and spend years maintaining projects that anyone can use. At the same time, other parts of technical culture can be dismissive, obsessed with status and hostile toward people who have not yet learned the unwritten rules.

Both things can exist simultaneously.

So I do not think the interesting question is whether programming is simply "open" or "closed."

The more interesting question is: open to whom?

A field can make its information publicly available while still making the process of acquiring real understanding unnecessarily difficult.

What a healthier culture would look like

A less zero-sum technical culture would not require lowering standards or pretending difficult concepts are easy.

It would simply separate expertise from ego.

Someone asking a basic question would not threaten the value of someone else's knowledge. Explaining a concept clearly would be considered evidence of understanding rather than a waste of time. Documentation would not merely be technically complete; it would also acknowledge the context a reader needs in order to use it.

Most importantly, another person's progress would not be treated as your loss.

Knowledge is one of the few resources that does not disappear when it is shared.

Maybe technical culture would be better if it acted like it.
